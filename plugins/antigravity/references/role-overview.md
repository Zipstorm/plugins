# Role overview

A role overview is the first page a recruiter sees for a new role: what the job
description asks, how many people meet it, what would widen that, and where to
search first. Build it straight from the job description without asking, in the
order below. Every figure comes from the job description, a count in this
conversation or a page read today; a figure with no such source is left out. A
smaller honest page is the goal.

## When it is done

Saving is a step, not the goal. Save after each stage so the recruiter watches the
page fill in, sending the whole overview each time with every part saved so far:

1. the requirements (section 1), as soon as the job description is read;
2. the pool as written and its ideas (sections 2 and 3), from one count;
3. pay and competing roles, with the pages read named in the notes (section 4);
4. the strategies (section 5).

Each save's answer says whether the overview is finished and what remains. It is
finished when every section holds measured or read content, or says why it does
not. A part that truly cannot be done is declared as left out, with its reason,
which the page shows; it is never filled with an estimate. Until the answer says
finished, keep going: never tell the recruiter the overview is done. The final
reply claims no more than was saved and names anything still missing or left out.

## 1. What the role asks

Sort what the job description asks of a candidate into five groups, as
[role criteria](criteria.md) reads a job description. A group it says nothing for
stays empty.

- **Must have**: what it states as required, and the place and working pattern.
- **Nice to have**: only what it calls preferred, a bonus or a plus.
- **Trade-off**: only where it says one thing excuses another, as one "If …, …" rule.
- **Avoid**: a background or trait it rules out.
- **Pay**: the stated range.

One item per stated requirement. A list given on one line stays one item
("JavaScript, PHP, React Native"). The listing's header (place, working pattern,
pay) is part of the job description. Duties and the company's description of
itself are not requirements; a trait it describes of the person is one only where
it sits with the requirements, and is listed once. Name each item in a few of the
job description's own words, keeping its numbers, and quote the line that
supports it exactly as written, inner quotation marks included; from a long
passage, quote its first sentence or two.

When your SeekOut tools can save candidate evaluations, mark up to six of them as
the evaluation criteria (at most three must-haves, two nice-to-haves and one thing
to avoid), as [criteria](criteria.md#the-evaluation-criteria) says: only ones a
profile can show and the search does not already settle ([evaluating
candidates](evaluating.md)). Name them when you save; a must-have no profile states
is named as not counted instead, judged when screening.

Where the client keeps the recruiter's [work log](work-log.md#default-criteria),
read its preferences before sorting, and add each default criterion to its group
beside the job description's own:

- Name it in at most 40 characters. Its supporting line is `Your default: ` and
  the recruiter's words, at most 240 characters in all; tighten longer words
  without changing their meaning.
- A default the job description already states is one requirement, with the job
  description's line.
- Employers never to source from are one thing to avoid ("Now at an off-limits
  employer"), however many entries list them. Its line names them where they
  fit, and says how many where they do not.
- Where a default and the job description disagree (it requires what a default
  rules out, names an off-limits employer as a target, or makes a default's
  must-have impossible), ask once which holds for this role, every conflict in
  one question, after the job description is saved and before this save. The
  answer is for this role only and changes no default. Nothing else is asked.
- A rubric keeps the first ten: when defaults push one of the job description's
  requirements out, say which.

With no log, or one that cannot be read, build from the job description alone.
The final reply names the defaults applied, in one sentence.

Save the requirements now, before counting anything.

## 2. The pool as written

Count one pool: the role's title, with only its direct equivalents, in the role's
place, plus each must-have that people write on a profile: a named skill, tool,
language or credential, or a number of years. Things required together are each
their own keyword, joined with AND, even when one line lists them; only stated
alternatives ("Go or Rust") share a group. Use at most three keyword groups, as
**sourcing** builds a search.

A must-have that profiles do not state (taste, pace, attitude, "fluency",
"ownership") gets no keyword. Name each one as not counted; it is judged when
screening. Never invent a keyword to stand in for one. Things to avoid are
applied when screening too, not in the count, with one exception: employers the
recruiter's defaults rule out. Exclude them in the pool as written, in every
idea's count and in every strategy's search, with the excluded-employer filter
for people working there now, one employer per value (the filter for anyone who
ever worked there only when the recruiter rules out former employees too). Never
name one as a target. Say which names the count could not match to an employer.

Count the pool as written and each idea (section 3) in the same call, then save
the pool, with the search it was counted from, and the ideas together.

## 3. Ideas to widen it

An idea is one change the recruiter can take to the hiring manager: one keyword
group, the titles, the place or one filter, never several at once. Count it as
the pool as written with that one thing changed; its gain is that count minus the
pool as written. Look in this order and keep at most four ideas in all, each with
a gain above zero:

1. **A stated trade-off**, applied. Where it loosens one requirement, count the
   pool with that requirement loosened. Where it excuses everything else for a
   group of people ("if they come from X, other gaps are fine"), count that group
   twice, with only the title and place, then with every keyword of the pool as
   written; its gain is the first count minus the second.
2. **More evidence for the same requirement**: skills or tools that prove the same
   ability, added to that keyword group; or other titles for the same job.
3. **Employers as evidence**: for a must-have people rarely write down, people at
   named employers where it is the daily work, counted without that keyword. Find
   the employers on the web (section 4) and name a few of them in the reason.
4. **A wider place the working pattern allows**: the commutable metro for an
   on-site or hybrid role, the time zone or country for a remote one. Never a
   place the job description rules out.
5. **One question for the hiring manager**, at most: a must-have the job
   description does not flex, counted without it and worded as a question ("Ask
   whether PHP is needed on day one"), never as a recommendation. Choose the one
   that adds the most people and that no other idea already loosens.

Say in each idea's reason which line of the job description it rests on. The
ideas are saved with the pool; a finished overview has at least two with a gain,
or says why not.

## 4. What to read on the web

The first count answers the pool's largest employers. With them in hand, read the
web for what SeekOut does not hold, in this order. Open the page: a search
result's summary is not a page read. Keep the site and the date for each fact.
After two pages that do not give a fact, move on and leave it out.

1. **Companies where the hardest must-have is the product.** Search for lists of
   such companies with people in the role's place, or hiring remotely where the
   role allows it ("AI companies in Seattle", a Built In or Crunchbase list, a news
   round-up). Take five to eight names from pages you read.
2. **Open job listings for this job in this metro.** One source answers four
   things. LinkedIn's public job search needs no sign-in
   (linkedin.com/jobs/search?keywords=<job title>&location=<metro>):
   - its heading states how many roles are open for this job in the metro;
   - its Company filter names the employers with the most of them: those are
     hiring heavily;
   - searched again as "<employer> <job title>" for each of the pool's four
     largest employers, it lists that employer's roles: count the ones from that
     employer whose title is this job, and mark the count "or more" when the page
     is full. Leave out an employer with none: a zero is not a competitor;
   - those titles name the teams ("Senior Product Designer, Design Systems").
     Take three to six teams or products whose work proves the hardest must-have.
   An employer's own careers search works too where it shows a count. A city's
   Built In job board shows each listing's posted pay range.
3. **Layoffs** among those employers since last year. The public WARN notices are
   the steadiest source (warntracker.com/company/<employer> gives the date of the
   latest notice); a news page that names the employer works too. Mark only what
   a page says.
4. **Market pay**, when the job description states pay. Follow pay research
   (**reporting**). Read one salary page for this job title and metro at
   the level the job description implies, and compare like with like:
   - a base range in the job description: a page that gives base pay, such as
     Built In's salary page for the title and city (average, median, lowest and
     highest base);
   - total pay, or no base page readable: levels.fyi's title-and-location page
     (25th, median and 75th percentile of total pay).
   Take the page's low, median and high for a year, say whether they are base or
   total pay, and say what they are: which percentiles, how many salaries. Prefer
   the 25th and 75th percentile to the lowest and highest salary reported. Turn
   an hourly range into a year at 2,080 hours and say so. Where
   no salary page can be read, use the posted ranges of at least three open
   listings instead: the lowest, median and highest midpoint.

Reply rates and time to hire are not searched: SeekOut keeps dated benchmarks and
applies them to the pool. Only name the role's function.

Save pay and competing roles, with the pages read in the notes. Where no page gives
them, declare the part left out with what was tried.

## 5. Where to search first

A strategy is a group of people worth going to before the rest, counted as its
own pool. Give two to four, each naming its targets and one line of evidence that
is a count or a page read. A target is an employer the count recognised, or a team
or product named on a page you read. Skip a strategy you cannot name targets for.

1. **Past applicants**, when an applicant system is connected: people who reached
   late rounds for similar roles.
2. **Teams whose work is the evidence**: the teams and products from the web.
   Count the role's titles and place at their employers, with the team or product
   names as keywords.
3. **Employers where a must-have is the product**: the companies from the web.
   Count the role's titles and place at those employers, without that
   requirement's keyword. Where an idea widens the place, use its place and say
   which idea it applies.
4. **Referrals**: no count. Ask for the names of the people on the team.

Each strategy's Search button runs its search, so attach that search to the
strategy: the query and filters exactly as you counted them, and its count as the
people figure. Attach the pool as written the same way. A figure must be the count
of the search attached to it; if you change a search after counting, count it
again before you save.

A strategy leaves out a keyword when the employer or team is the evidence for it,
so its count can be larger than the pool as written. A strategy that is only the
pool as written with one more filter is not a strategy; it is the same people.
For its evidence line, count where its people came from or went: the same titles
at one group of targets who previously worked at the other. Save the strategies; a
finished overview has at least two that carry their counted search. Name each
one's targets and evidence line wherever you can; a strategy with none to name
(past applicants) says so in its reason.

## 6. Write it

Write for an executive reader. Lead with the conclusion and the number, then the
evidence. Use short declarative sentences and plain verbs. No hedging, no filler,
no jargon, no "we believe" and no restating the job description. A title names
the action in three to eight words ("Open to remote in Pacific time"). A reason
is one or two sentences: what changes, then what was counted or read.

Every text field has a character limit, and the page is designed around it. Write
to fit the first time. If a save comes back with parts that run over, rewrite
those parts more tightly with the same meaning and figures; never cut a sentence
off or drop a figure to make room.

Copy every count exactly as it was answered and never add two counts together.
Leave out any field you have no sourced value for. Pay and competing roles are
each an idea of their own: give each a title and a reason, using only what the
job description offers. Mark a target as hiring or as having layoffs only from
the pages above. In the notes, name the pages read with the date, and say in a
sentence or two what was left out and why.
